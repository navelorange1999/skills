# Imaginative Literature

**What makes them distinct:** Imaginative works create experiences;
expository works convey knowledge. Do not read a novel the way you read an
argument. The test of a good novel is whether the imagined world is
coherent and moving, not whether its propositions are true.

## Rule modifications (apply to all fiction)

- **Rule 1 (classify):** Further classify — is it a novel, a play, a poem, a
  story cycle? Each subgenre has its own expectations.
- **Rule 2 (unity):** Unity in fiction is the *plot* — the shape of the
  story from beginning through middle to end. State the plot in a sentence
  or two.
- **Rule 3 (enumerate parts):** Parts of a story are its episodes and the
  ways they advance the plot; parts of a poem are its stanzas or movements.
- **Rule 5 (terms):** Terms in fiction are *characters, settings, and key
  images* — the recurring, named elements. A novel's "terms" are its named
  characters and places.
- **Rule 6 (propositions):** Fiction doesn't *assert* propositions in the
  logical sense. What it "says" is *shown* through incident and character.
- **Rule 7 (arguments):** The "argument" of a story is the sequence by
  which its events produce their cumulative effect.

## Don'ts

- **Don't resist the imagined world.** Let the author's world in; don't
  reject the premises because they aren't factual.
- **Don't look for propositions that aren't there.** A novel is not a
  coded lecture. If you find yourself extracting a "message," you are
  usually either projecting or reading didactic fiction (which is its own
  sub-genre and rarely the best literature).
- **Don't judge the author's world by your world.** Judge it by whether it
  is coherent on its own terms, and whether it moves you.

## Critical-stage

- Criticize on aesthetic grounds — is the world coherent, are the
  characters believable within it, does the plot satisfy?
- If you must disagree with the author's *view of human nature* or moral
  vision, do so only after experiencing the work on its terms.

## Subgenre-specific rules

### Stories (novels, novellas, short stories)

- Read a story *as a story*. The first pass should be for immersion, not
  analysis. Let yourself be carried along.
- Only after experiencing the whole do you go back for analytical reading.
- Unity = plot. Key terms = characters. The question "what is the story
  about?" is answered by telling the plot.

### Plays

- A play is written to be performed, not read. When reading, *imagine* the
  performance — the pace, the physical action, what's said versus shown.
- Shakespeare wrote most of his effect into the spaces between lines.
  Don't just read the words.

### Poems

- **Read the whole poem through at one sitting without stopping**, even if
  you don't understand it. Poems are experienced as wholes before they
  are understood.
- **Read the poem aloud.** Rhythm, sound, and pause are part of the
  meaning, not decoration.
- *Then* read it again, more carefully, attending to the key images, the
  turns of argument, the rhetorical structure.
- A difficult poem is often not obscure; it is compressed. Unpack it
  slowly — but only after you have felt its whole shape.
