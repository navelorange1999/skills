# History

**What makes it distinct:** A history book is a narrative of past events
shaped by a historian's judgment. It is neither pure fact nor pure
interpretation; it is both, inseparably. The historian has *selected* what
to include and *emphasized* what they think matters.

## Rule modifications

- **Rule 1 (classify):** What kind of history? Narrative, analytical,
  biographical, economic, social, intellectual? Each has conventions.
- **Rule 2 (unity):** The unity of a history is its thesis about the
  period — what the historian thinks the story *is*.
- **Rule 5 (terms):** Watch for period-specific terms and contested
  historical terms ("revolution," "empire," "reform"). The historian's
  definitions shape what counts as evidence.
- **Rule 8 (solved vs. unsolved):** Histories rarely resolve all questions
  about a period. Note what the historian leaves open — and what they
  don't know they've left open.

## Critical-stage pitfalls

- **Selection bias is inevitable.** What the historian *doesn't* mention
  shapes your picture as much as what they do. The four grounds for
  disagreement here often lodge at *incomplete analysis*.
- **Read at least two histories of any period you care about.** No single
  history is neutral; syntopical reading is the norm for historical
  inquiry, not the exception.
- **Beware the period-novel style.** Some histories read like novels.
  That's fine as prose, but check: is the vivid detail sourced, or is it
  imaginative reconstruction?
